Chapter 1

Cells and the cell theory

A cell is the basic unit of life. It is the smallest thing that can do everything a living thing does: take in energy, grow, respond, and reproduce.

The three principles of the cell theory

  1. All living things are made of cells. Some have one cell, some have trillions.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things. It is the smallest living unit.
  3. All cells come from other cells that already exist.

Who found cells?

Robert Hooke, 1665

He looked at a thin slice of cork with a microscope and saw rows of tiny empty boxes. He was the first person to use the name cell. What he saw were the walls of dead plant cells.

Anton van Leeuwenhoek, 1670s

He made his own microscopes with very strong lenses and was the first to see living cells: bacteria and other tiny organisms in pond water, and also blood cells. He called them animalcules.

About 170 years later Schleiden showed that all plants are made of cells, Schwann showed the same for animals, and Virchow added that cells come only from other cells. Together these ideas became the cell theory.

Chapter 2

Two kinds of cells

Prokaryotic cells

  • No nucleus. The DNA sits in a region called the nucleoid.
  • No membrane-bound organelles.
  • Small, about 1 to 10 µm.
  • Always single-celled: bacteria and archaea.
  • Divide by binary fission.

Eukaryotic cells

  • Have a nucleus that holds the DNA.
  • Have membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria, ER and Golgi.
  • Larger, about 10 to 100 µm.
  • Single-celled or many-celled: animals, plants, fungi, protists.
  • Divide by mitosis.

What both kinds share: DNA, a plasma membrane and ribosomes. Both also have cytoplasm.

Plant cell or animal cell?

Only in plant cells

  • Cell wall
  • Chloroplasts
  • One large central vacuole

Only in animal cells

  • Centrioles
  • Lysosomes (rare in plants)

Is a dividing cell a prokaryote or a eukaryote?

Look for a nucleus, for chromosomes that have thickened into X shapes, and for a mitotic spindle pulling them apart. If you see these, the cell is a eukaryote doing mitosis. A prokaryote has none of them. It is much smaller, has one circular chromosome, and simply copies its DNA and splits in two by binary fission.

Chapter 3

Organelles, with and without a membrane

An organelle is a small structure inside a cell that has its own job. Many organelles are wrapped in a membrane, which makes a separate room where one kind of chemistry can happen without disturbing the rest. Others have no membrane at all.

Double membrane

Nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts. Mitochondria and chloroplasts also carry their own DNA.

Single membrane

Rough ER, smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles.

No membrane

Ribosomes, nucleolus, centrioles, cytoskeleton. In bacteria also the nucleoid. These are made of protein or RNA packed together, with nothing wrapped around them.

Ribosomes have no membrane, so even a prokaryote can have them. That is why ribosomes are found in every cell.

Chapter 4

The plasma membrane

The plasma membrane (also called the cell membrane) is the thin boundary around every cell. It regulates the movement of substances into and out of the cell. It lets needed things in, lets wastes out, and keeps the inside of the cell stable. This balance is called homeostasis. The membrane also carries markers that let cells recognize each other.

One phospholipid

The whole membrane

In water, phospholipids line up in two layers with the heads facing the water and the tails hidden in the middle. This is the lipid bilayer. Proteins float in it like boats, so scientists call it the fluid mosaic model.

Integral proteins

Set into the bilayer. When one goes all the way through, it is a transmembrane protein. Channels, carriers and pumps are of this type.

Peripheral proteins

Sit on the surface of the membrane, inside or outside, and do not go through it.

Glycoproteins and glycolipids

A glycoprotein is a protein with a carbohydrate chain attached. A glycolipid is a lipid with a carbohydrate chain attached. They stick out on the outside of the cell and work as name tags for cell to cell recognition.

Selectively permeable

The membrane lets some things cross and blocks others.

Cross easily

Small molecules with no charge, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide. They slip between the tails.

Have difficulty crossing

Large molecules (glucose, proteins, starch), ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) and other polar or charged molecules. The middle of the bilayer is hydrophobic and pushes them back, so they need a transport protein.

If a cell lost its selective permeability, anything could go in or out. Harmful substances would enter, and nutrients, ions and water would leak. The cell could not keep homeostasis and would die.

Chapter 5

Transport across the membrane

A solute is a substance that is dissolved. The solvent is the fluid that dissolves it, such as water. Together they make a solution. A concentration gradient is a difference in concentration between two places.

Passive transport needs no energy

  • Diffusion. Molecules or ions move from higher to lower concentration, down the gradient. It needs no energy and stops when the distribution is equal.
  • Facilitated diffusion. Also down the gradient with no energy, but through a transport protein. Used by glucose and ions.
  • Osmosis. Diffusion of water through a selectively permeable membrane.

Active transport needs energy

A protein pump uses ATP to move a substance against its gradient, from lower to higher concentration. The best known example is the sodium-potassium pump, which pushes 3 sodium ions out and 2 potassium ions in.

The bus on the hill. A bus can roll downhill with the engine off. That is passive transport. To carry passengers uphill it must burn fuel. The bus is the protein pump, the passengers are the molecules, the fuel is ATP, and uphill means against the concentration gradient.

Hypertonic, hypotonic, isotonic

These words describe the solution around a cell compared with the inside of the cell. Water moves by osmosis toward the side with more solute.

Moving big loads with vesicles

Endocytosis: into the cell

The membrane folds around material and pinches off a vesicle.

  • Phagocytosis, cell eating: the cell engulfs large particles, such as a bacterium.
  • Pinocytosis, cell drinking: the cell takes in droplets of the fluid outside.

Exocytosis: out of the cell

A vesicle made by the Golgi apparatus fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside. Materials leave the cell this way.

Both use energy, because the cell has to move and reshape its membrane.

Chapter 6

Why cells stay small

Everything a cell needs comes in through its surface, and wastes leave the same way. The volume is the part that uses food and makes waste. When a cell grows, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so each piece of membrane has to serve more and more cytoplasm.

A large surface area to volume ratio means materials reach every part of the cell quickly. When the ratio gets too small, the cell divides. Two small cells have more surface than one big cell.

Chapter 7

The cell cycle and mitosis

The cell cycle is the life of a cell, from the moment it forms until it divides into two. Remember the order with IPMATC: Interphase, Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase, Cytokinesis.

Interphase: getting ready

  • G1. The cell grows, makes proteins and new organelles, and does its normal job.
  • S. The DNA is copied. Each chromosome now has two identical halves called sister chromatids.
  • G2. The cell grows more, checks the copied DNA, and makes what it needs to divide.

A cell spends most of its life, about 90%, in interphase. The M phase is short.

M phase: two major events

Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.

The mitotic spindle is a set of microtubule fibers. It attaches to the chromosomes and separates the sister chromatids during mitosis.

Mitosis makes two daughter cells that are genetically identical to each other and to the parent cell.

Cytokinesis in animal and plant cells

Animal cell

The membrane pinches inward in the middle. This groove is the cleavage furrow. It gets deeper until the cell is cut in two.

Plant cell

The stiff cell wall cannot pinch. Vesicles from the Golgi line up in the middle and join into a cell plate, which grows outward and becomes the new cell wall.

The result is the same in both: the cytoplasm is divided and there are two cells, each with its own nucleus.

G0: cells that stop dividing

Some cells leave the cycle after G1 and enter a resting stage called G0. Mature nerve cells and muscle cells stay in G0. They keep doing their job but they do not divide.

Why do eukaryotic cells divide?

  1. Growth. A body grows by adding cells, not by making its cells bigger.
  2. Repair. New cells close a cut or heal a broken bone.
  3. Replacement. Old and dead cells, such as skin and blood cells, are replaced.

Binary fission

Bacteria have no nucleus and do not do mitosis. A bacterium copies its single circular chromosome, grows longer, and splits in the middle into two identical cells. This process is binary fission.

Chapter 8

When division gets out of control

Checkpoints in the cell cycle tell a cell when to divide and when to stop. Cancer begins when changes in the DNA, called mutations, break this control and cells keep dividing. The extra cells can form a lump called a tumor.

Benign tumor

  • Stays in one place.
  • Does not invade the tissue around it.
  • Is not cancer and can usually be removed.

Malignant tumor

  • Is cancer.
  • Invades and damages the tissue around it.
  • Cells can break away and spread to other parts of the body. This is called metastasis.

Drugs that interfere with mitosis

Cancer cells divide much more often than most healthy cells. A drug that blocks mitosis, for example by stopping the spindle from forming, stops them from dividing. The tumor stops growing and its cells die. The same drugs also hit healthy cells that divide often, such as hair roots and the lining of the stomach, and this explains side effects like hair loss.
